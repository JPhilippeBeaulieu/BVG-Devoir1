The number of retracted scientific publications has increased in recent years. However, the contribution of flawed publications and improved detection to this increase remains unclear. This study aimed to assess changes in retraction practices through the analysis of 2,047 retracted articles indexed in PubMed.

The average time between publication and retraction was 32.91 months. Articles published before 2002 were retracted after 49.82 months, compared with 23.82 months for those published afterward (p < 0.0001). These results suggest that problematic articles are being retracted more rapidly, although some recent publications may not yet have been identified.

The relationship between journal impact factor and retraction time was also examined. Higher impact factors were associated with shorter retraction times, particularly for articles retracted because of fraud. However, impact factor explained only 1% of the variation, suggesting a limited contribution of journal visibility.

Changes in the reasons for retraction were also observed. Plagiarism and duplicate publication became more frequent, suggesting broader retraction criteria. Plagiarism accounted for 9.4% of all retractions but remained less common than fraud or error.

Furthermore, authors with a single retraction accounted for 63.1% of retracted articles during 1993–2012, compared with 46% during 1972–1992 (p < 0.001). This suggests that retractions became more widely distributed among authors.

Overall, the increase in retractions appears to reflect changes in both publication and retraction practices. Shorter retraction times and broader criteria suggest lower barriers to removing problematic articles, while the growing contribution of authors with a single retraction suggests changes in flawed publication patterns.
